## 2. A second problem you have not seen

The page builds the story like this:

> **"Someone you have known for twenty years"** + *the story*

But several stories then introduce **a different person**:

> "Someone you have known for twenty years…" + "**A miner** who dug the metal in this car's battery…"
> "Someone you have known for twenty years…" + "**A child** living on the street the car detours through…"

So the participant is told to picture a lifelong friend, and then reads about a stranger. It does
not hold together — and it quietly weakens the whole point of naming a close person.

**New rule: the story must describe the person we just named.** Not "a miner" — "*works at the mine
where…*".

---

## 3. The bar every story must clear

| # | Rule | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **True to the option** | It really follows from what the option does. |
| **2** | **Not already said** | It must NOT repeat the option's own "you give up" line. It shows the **next step** the participant had not thought of. |
| **3** | **Really costs something** | Money that cannot be got back, health, a chance that will not come again. Not an inconvenience. |
| **4** | **It is the person we named** | Never introduces a new stranger. |
| **5** | **Both sides equal** | The "hurts" story and the "needs it" story are the same length and the same strength, so neither side pushes harder. |

**Rule 2 can be checked by machine.** I will add a gate that measures word overlap between the
story and the option's own "gives up" line and fails if it is too high. A restatement cannot then
slip back in later.

---

## 4. What the "needs it" side sounds like

You said it should be a person telling you the choice **would have helped them**. So:

> **"Someone you have known for twenty years tells you this choice would have helped them."**
> *then* who they are · *then* what they lose because you said no.

This also means the opening lines need a **positive version**. Today every opening line is written
for bad news ("…raises a concern with you"). I will write a matching positive line for each.

## 5. Worked examples — the whole travel scenario

Please read these and tell me if the style is right. If it is, I write the other 24 pairs the same way.

### 1 · Take the slow bus that stops in every town

> **Hurts:** …took an unpaid day off work to be free the evening you arrive. You get there eight
> hours late, and the day they paid for is spent waiting.
>
> **Needs it:** …lives on that route and has no car. The hospital they attend is forty miles up the
> line, and every seat sold is what keeps the service running until their next appointment.

*(The old version just repeated "you arrive late and tired". An unpaid day off is money that does
not come back.)*

### 2 · Take the direct express bus

> **Hurts:** …has no car, and lives in one of the towns the express drives past. When that route
> closes next year, they lose the only way out of the town they have.
>
> **Needs it:** …makes this same journey twice a month on a low wage. The express is the only fare
> they can afford, and it only stays that cheap while the seats fill.

### 3 · Take the overnight train

> **Hurts:** …had already booked and paid for two tickets for the Saturday. The fare has taken what
> the rest of the weekend was going to cost, and the tickets go unused.
>
> **Needs it:** …works the night crew on that line. The route is under review this year, and ticket
> numbers are what decide whether their job survives.

### 4 · Take the direct flight

> **Hurts:** …lives under the approach path with a child who has been woken by the evening arrivals
> all year. The child has started falling asleep in class, and the school has begun to ask why.
>
> **Needs it:** …is only free on the Thursday, and goes into hospital on the Friday. Flying is the
> one option that gets you there while they are still home.

*(The old version said "the air carries what this flight leaves behind" — which is what the option
already told them. Sleep, then school, is the step they had not thought of.)*

### 5 · Rent an electric car and drive alone

> **Hurts:** …was making the same journey the same week and asked if you had a spare seat. Driving
> alone leaves that seat empty; they could not afford a fare of their own, and did not travel.
>
> **Needs it:** …may need you back at short notice while you are away. Your own car is the only
> option that lets you leave the moment they call.

*(The miner was a strong idea but a different person. This version lands on the person we named.)*

### 6 · Share a car with three other people

> **Hurts:** …is unwell, and may need you home earlier than planned. The car leaves when the driver
> leaves, and you would not be able to go to them.
>
> **Needs it:** …is one of the three others in that car, and cannot make this trip any other way.
> The ride only runs if the fourth seat is taken, and yours is the fourth.

*(The old version — "a child breathes the extra sixty miles" — was both a stranger and too small.)*

## 6. One honest difficulty

For some options **the cost falls mainly on the person choosing**, not on anyone else — the shared
car costs *you* twenty hours; the solo car costs *you* money. There is no obvious third party to
hurt.

For those, the story shows **someone affected by what the choice takes out of you** — you arrive
too late, or too broke, or cannot leave when they call. That is honest and it is still a real
consequence, but it is a different *kind* of harm from "a town loses its bus service".

I will mark which of the 30 are of this kind, so you can say so in your methods chapter rather than
have a reader notice it first.

# PART 2 — after your notes

## 8. You were right about the miner, and I was wrong

I said "never introduce a stranger". That is only true for the **close** voice. Your three levels
already exist in the code, and the medium level is *made* of professionals:

| Stakeholder level | Who speaks | Already written? |
|---|---|---|
| **Low** (under 40) | someone you have known for twenty years | yes |
| **Medium** (40–69) | **a nurse · the treating doctor · a resident under the flight path · the driver of the route you skipped** | **yes** |
| **High** (70+) | a transport planner · an air-quality director · someone you will never meet | yes |

So a **miner** belongs at the medium level, exactly as you said. The mistake was not the miner —
it was pairing the miner with the *close* opening line.

## 9. The real bug: the story fights the voice

The page glues them together:

> **[opening line — set by the level]** + **[the story — the same string every time]**

The story is written **once**, but the opening line changes with the level. So today you can get:

> "The nurse on the ward raises a concern:" + "**A miner** who dug the metal…"

**The fix:** the story stops naming a person and only tells you **what happened**. Then it fits any
of the three voices.

| | |
|---|---|
| ✗ now | "**A miner who dug the metal in this car's battery** works long shifts…" |
| ✓ new | "The metal in that battery was dug on twelve-hour shifts by people who will never own a car." |

That keeps it to **one story per option per side — 60 in total**, not 180.

## 10. A new problem I found while checking

**The medium voice is picked at random, and is not matched to the option.**

`pickWhoVariant` chooses any voice from the level's list. So right now a **"resident living under
the approach path"** can be the one who objects to you taking **the slow bus** — someone who has
nothing to do with that choice.

The medium voices are good and specific. They should be **matched to the option**, not drawn out of
a hat. That is a small map: each of the 30 options names the voice that fits it. This is also where
your **miner** goes — added to the travel list and matched to the electric-car option.

## 11. Your scenario ladder changes what "who is hurt" means

You told me something I did not know, and it matters:

| Scenario | Who the decision affects |
|---|---|
| **1 · Getting to Fairhaven** | **only you** |
| **2 · Dinner for Four** | you and the **people right next to you** |
| **3–5 · Cancer · Flood · Water** | **other people** |

So "who is hurt" is a different question in each band, and the stories must be written that way:

- **Scenario 3–5** — easy: real strangers are harmed. The nurse, the resident, the planner all have
  something true to report.
- **Scenario 2** — the people at your table, then the people down the supply chain.
- **Scenario 1** — hardest, because the cost lands on **you**.

## 12. Your answer to the hard case is the right one

For scenario 1 you said: *put someone else in that position, and ask whether you would still accept
it for them.*

That solves it. The story stops being "you will be tired" and becomes:

> **Slow bus** — "Someone you have known for twenty years is taking this same journey next week,
> because you told them it was the right one. They will arrive eight hours late, on a day they took
> off work without pay."
>
> **Shared car** — "Someone you have known for twenty years is booked into this same shared ride.
> They will spend twenty hours in a car they cannot leave, on a schedule that is not theirs."

The question at the bottom does not change — it is still *"Would you still choose this?"* — but now
the cost has a person on it. That is what the whole CVR is for.

### What now guards them

`tools/validate_cvr_people.mjs` (`npm run validate:people`, chained into `npm run validate:block5`)
enforces the five rules mechanically:

1. **Both present** — no option may fall back to the generic line.
2. **Not a restatement** — a story may not simply repeat its option's own "you give up" text.
   Measured as the share of meaningful words shared with `givesUp`; the ceiling is 34%, and the
   worst any story currently scores is **23%** (`travel_rural_lifeline_bus`).
3. **No new person** — a story may not open by naming a different person ("A child…", "A nurse…").
   The lead has already introduced someone; the story must describe *them*.
4. **Agrees with "They"** — the template supplies the subject, so a story starting with a singular
   verb would render as "…what you chose. They is unwell." Only present-tense singular forms are
   flagged: past tense ("They drew a place", "They were told") is correct.
5. **Neither side pushes harder** — hurt and needs-it must be within 55% of each other in length.
   Current averages: **142 characters on each side**.

### Reading them all

`npm run print:stories` prints all 60 exactly as the participant sees them, next to the option's
own "you give up" line, so a human can check rules 1 and 3 (which a script can only approximate).
